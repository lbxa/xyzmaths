# Chapter 7. Riemann integration {id="ch:analysis-i:riemann-integration"}

## 7.1 Integration {id="sec:analysis-i:integration"}

Integration will compare accumulated quantities with finite sums, leading to a precise condition for their limit. [^49]

:::notes{id="footnotes-48"}

[^49]: Riemann introduced his integrability framework in his 1854 study of trigonometric representation. Dedekind published the work posthumously in 1867 ([Riemann 1867, secs. 4–6](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Riemann1867)). Thus the integral grew partly from a question about which functions Fourier series could represent. It broadened integration beyond continuous functions and shaped real analysis. See [Figure 7.1](/notes/maths-analysis/analysis-i/riemann-integration/#fig:portrait:riemann).

:::

## 7.2 Upper and lower sums {id="sec:analysis-i:upper-and-lower-sums"}

Upper and lower sums bound an accumulated quantity from above and below on each interval of a partition. [^50]

:::notes{id="footnotes-49"}

[^50]: Darboux’s 1875 *Mémoire sur les fonctions discontinues* develops upper and lower integrals  ([Darboux 1875](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Darboux1875)). These bounds make integrability a question of whether the gap can be made arbitrarily small. This viewpoint clarifies Riemann’s theory and the role of discontinuities, and provides a practical way to prove error estimates. See [Figure 7.2](/notes/maths-analysis/analysis-i/riemann-integration/#fig:portrait:darboux).

## 7.5 Fundamental theorem of calculus {id="sec:analysis-i:fundamental-theorem-of-calculus"}

The fundamental theorem will connect integration, which accumulates quantities, with differentiation, which measures local change. [^51]

:::notes{id="footnotes-50"}

[^51]: Newton’s *De analysi* (1669) computes areas using series and rules relating powers to quadratures ([Newton 1669](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Newton1669)). Together with the differential calculus published by Leibniz  ([Leibniz 1684](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Leibniz1684)), this belongs to the development that made differentiation and integration a unified method. The modern theorem adds explicit continuity and differentiability hypotheses. Its applications include mechanics and differential equations.

:::
