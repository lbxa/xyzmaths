# Chapter 2. Metric spaces and topology {id="ch:analysis-i:metric-spaces-and-topology"}

## 2.1 Metric spaces {id="sec:analysis-i:metric-spaces"}

A metric abstracts the distance properties used on the real line, so that convergence can also be studied for points, sequences, and functions. [^22]

:::notes{id="footnotes-21"}

[^22]: Fréchet’s 1906 thesis studies convergence in collections of mathematical objects, including functions ([Fréchet 1906](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Frechet1906)). Its abstract distance framework helped move analysis beyond individual real or complex variables. Metric spaces provide a common language for approximation, continuity, and functional analysis. See [Figure 2.1](/notes/maths-analysis/analysis-i/metric-spaces-and-topology/#fig:portrait:frechet).

## 2.4 Heine–Borel theorem {id="sec:analysis-i:heine-borel-theorem"}

The Heine–Borel theorem connects a covering property with closedness and boundedness in finite-dimensional Euclidean space. [^23]

:::notes{id="footnotes-22"}

[^23]: Heine’s 1872 treatment uses finite interval coverings. Borel’s 1895 thesis uses a covering argument in function theory  ([Heine 1872](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Heine1872); [Borel 1895](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Borel1895)). The modern compactness theorem grew through these related steps. Its impact is to reduce infinitely many local requirements to finitely many choices. Closed and bounded sets need not be compact in an arbitrary metric space. See [Figure 2.2](/notes/maths-analysis/analysis-i/metric-spaces-and-topology/#fig:portrait:borel).
