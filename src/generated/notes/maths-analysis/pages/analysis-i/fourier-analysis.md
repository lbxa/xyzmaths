# Chapter 9. Fourier analysis {id="ch:analysis-i:fourier-analysis"}

## 9.1 Fourier series {id="sec:analysis-i:fourier-series"}

Fourier series represent functions using trigonometric modes. The representation and its convergence require separate questions. [^54]

:::notes{id="footnotes-53"}

[^54]: Fourier’s *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* (1822) uses trigonometric expansions to solve heat-conduction problems  ([Fourier 1822](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Fourier1822)). The physical problem was to follow an initial temperature distribution over time. This programme connected analysis with partial differential equations and became foundational for frequency methods in physics and signal analysis. See [Figure 9.1](/notes/maths-analysis/analysis-i/fourier-analysis/#fig:portrait:fourier).

:::

## 9.2 Convergence of Fourier series {id="sec:analysis-i:convergence-of-fourier-series"}

A formal trigonometric expansion needs hypotheses before it can be identified with the function it is meant to represent. [^55]

:::notes{id="footnotes-54"}

[^55]: Dirichlet’s 1829 paper establishes convergence under specific conditions and examines shortcomings in earlier arguments  ([Dirichlet 1829](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Dirichlet1829)). Riemann later revisited representation and integration ([Riemann 1867](/notes/maths-analysis/references/#ref-Riemann1867)). This sequence of problems explains why Fourier analysis helped drive more careful definitions of function, continuity, and convergence.

:::
